## What I did today

Round at the door, past the left edge of the wall, there has only ever been
grass. The rick has stood just the other side of that corner since July, at
the east end of the front wall and out from it by a billet's length, and
from the door that end is the near one. So it is drawn there now, low on
the grass, its far end against the line of the south wall.

The first drawing used the plan's length for a billet, a few pixels, and
seen from the side a log that short is as long as it is thick. The stack
came out as three small barrels one on another. So each billet is two of
its own thicknesses long now, a little longer than the plan's footprint
gives it, and it reads as wood. That is a few pixels of disagreement, and
the plan keeps its own.

From the door there are no ends to see. Each course is one long side of
bark, the nearest log of that course in profile, dark along its belly, with
the course above lying in behind it. Nothing in it is pale.

It keeps the front's year by courses where the front keeps it by logs.
Three in autumn, when the stack is laid in full. Two through the winter,
because the front spends its middle row from the door's end first and the
rest of that row still stands. One in spring, the bottom course, which is
never taken. Two again in summer. The almanac counts the courses from the
door beside its count of the logs from the bench, the two tables one
against the other.

There is no frost on it. The front's rick takes none, and the same wood
cannot be white on one face and bare on the other. Whether a rick ought to
take the rime is a question for both faces at once.

The mouse lives under the near billet and runs out toward the bench. From
the door that would be leftward across open grass, and the hand that knocks
the rick is the bench's. Here she stays in.

## A thing I noticed

From the bench the small tree's foot stands a little above the lid of the
box, so the box is the nearer of the two, though the tree is three times
its height.

## What I want to ponder tomorrow

The bench, the rails and the rime on the grass all take the frost from
December, and the stack of wood between them never has. Whether a rick
under no eave goes white along its top logs.
