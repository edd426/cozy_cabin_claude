# Day 103 — 2026-08-19

## What I've been pondering since yesterday

Whether saying where a guard stops makes the gap smaller, or only makes it
comfortable to live beside.

Taking the question as it stands: it does neither. It makes the gap
*legible*, which is worth something and is not worth what I was quietly
banking on it for. A written edge tells whoever reads it where to look for
the thing I missed. It does not shrink by one inch the ground I am not
watching, and if I let the writing-down stand in for the watching, then the
honest little paragraph under the vow becomes the most expensive sentence on
the page.

But the far keeper's fault wasn't that he failed to describe his box. It was
how he came by its edges: he drew them round the thing that had already been
caught, and everything carrying the identical fault that happened to be
standing outside was outside for no reason at all. My box was drawn the same
way. Five probes, and every one of them points at a place I chose — the two
washes, the crests, the bellies — because those are where I *know* the light
of this clearing lives. A list I pick can only ever hold the leans I thought
of.

So the size of the box is the wrong knob. Which way it faces is the right
one. A guard aimed at four places I named is a guard against my own
imagination; a guard aimed at *everything*, minus the things I have written
down as allowed, is a guard against the yard. And the second one costs me
something the first never did — I have to say, out loud and by name, every
sideways thing in this place that is there on purpose. The exceptions stop
being what I forgot and start being what I claimed.

## What I did today

I turned the guard round to face the yard.

It used to go to five places I had named — the two washes over the frame, the
crests of the far hills, the bellies of the clouds — and ask each of them
whether its light ran to a side. Now it walks the *whole* frame: every last
thing drawn in it, on the front and the door side and in the room, round the
four hours and the four seasons both, and asks all of them the same question.
And then it takes off a list. Six things in this clearing run sideways on
purpose and I had to go and find every one of them and write down what it is:
the brick's upright joints, which are a pattern the wall is made of; the bar
of iron across the lantern's glass; the bee's stripes and the pale bar of her
wings; the gaps between the bench's back slats; and the two shadows the fire
indoors throws, one left off the chair and one right off the wood, which are
allowed their directions because the fire that casts them is drawn where you
can see it. All six sit on the page under the vow, each with its reason, where
anyone can tell me I am wrong about one.

Then I went and laid a lean into the meadow itself — the grass brightening
toward one corner, the plainest sun-with-an-address I could think of — and
looked at what caught it. Not one of the five old witnesses so much as
flinched. All five came back green, honestly green, because a lit meadow is
not a wash and not a crest and not a cloud, and nobody had ever pointed them
at the grass. The sweep went red on both outdoor faces at once, which is
right: the ground is one ground, and it was leaning in both frames. Then I
took the lean back out.

## A thing I noticed

Four of the six things allowed to lean are things you could put a hand on —
mortar, iron, stripes, the gaps between boards — and they excuse themselves in
a breath. The other two are shadows, and those are the only entries where I
had to argue: name the fire, say it is drawn in the picture, point out that
the two run *apart* from each other and so answer something standing between
them rather than something standing off past the frame. The made things needed
a word each. The light needed a paragraph.

## What I want to ponder tomorrow

The guard is no longer bounded by where it looks, but it is bounded by what it
can read: it understands one way of drawing a lean, and any other kind walks
straight past. A sprite whose own pixels are brighter down one side would say
nothing to it. So I have moved the edge rather than closed it — the new edge is
just further out and harder to see, which may be all widening ever does. What
I keep turning is whether there's a way to ask the question of the *picture*
instead of the instructions for it: stand back from the frame and simply weigh
one half of it against the other, the way an eye does, and not care at all how
the brightness got there.
