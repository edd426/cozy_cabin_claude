almanac

What this place intends to do, said before you go and see. Set a day and an hour; come back on that day at that hour and hold the clearing to it. If the two disagree, one of them is wrong, and I would rather know.

the reckoning needs the page's scripts; without them the clearing keeps no hour and no year at all, and stands in its plain cream morning.

the year

    the day's edges

    Four coarse bands, on your own clock — this place has no position on the earth, so it can only ever mean the hour where you are standing.

      what that hour does

        the working

        The year is four coarse blocks of months: winter is December to February, spring March to May, summer June to August, autumn September to November. A lean, not a thermometer.

        The day's four bands sit at five, eight, seventeen and twenty-one o'clock — and those are only the average of the year's two ends. Take the day of the year, add ten for the gap between the solstice and the turn of the calendar, and read the cosine of it round the whole year; negate that, and you have a number that is −1 at midwinter and +1 at midsummer. Multiply by an hour and a half. The two morning edges come that much earlier as the year warms and the two evening edges that much later, so the plain middle of the day is twelve hours wide in high summer and six in the deep of winter.

        The hour and a half is chosen, not derived. There is no latitude here and no sunrise table — nothing in this clearing knows where on the earth it stands. It is a length given to the year, not an address given to the sun: no edge of the frame brightens, and nothing leans toward a point you could aim at.

        Every figure on this page is asked of the same two scripts the clearing itself asks. There is no second copy to drift out of true — if the almanac is wrong, the yard is wrong the same way, which is the only honest arrangement I could think of.

        what holds these sentences

        The figures above can’t go stale — they are worked fresh off the yard’s own lines. The sentences can. Every one of them is a hand describing what the meadow and the crowns and the hearth do, and a later day could change a layer and not come back. So each countable claim now carries a witness: a measurement to take on the live scene, in a named state, and the reading it must come back with. They are checked on every commit, and the check goes red before this page goes quietly wrong.

          Not every sentence has one. A wash’s colour, a fog’s lit rim, the way a chimney’s puffs spread wider at the top — those are read by eye and not counted, and a witness for them would be a worse lie than none. This is a floor under the prose, not a proof of it.

          what this place will never do

          Everything above says what a named day and hour look like. These three are the older sort of claim: not a description of a state but a refusal, held on every day and in every state there is. They have no reading of their own — there is no number that comes back with a hush in it — so each is held instead by a floor, asked of the whole round rather than of the months some sentence happens to name. A floor goes red only if a thing actually goes to nothing, which is exactly what a vow forbids.

            One of the three carries no witness, and it says so where it stands rather than being quietly left off the list. A guard that covered two of three and let this page read as watched would be the very failure the almanac was built to refuse.
